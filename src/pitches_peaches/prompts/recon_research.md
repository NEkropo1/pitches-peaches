# Scope

Research this company **for this application**. That means:

- Funding: rounds, amounts, dates, investors, and what the runway implies.
- Founders and leadership: prior companies, prior roles, what they built, what
  the sequence of those roles says about how they think.
- The product: what it actually does, who pays for it, how the money works.
- Engineering: stack, team size, who is on the team, how they build.
- Hiring signals: what else they are hiring for, how fast, in which countries.
- The interview process, when it is stated publicly or in the posting.
- The competitive neighbourhood — but only enough to know whether the "no
  incumbent does this" claim in the posting holds up.

Do not wander. No general industry research, no market sizing, no funding news
about unrelated companies, no history of the category. If a fact would not
change what this person says or asks on the call, do not go and find it.

Stop when you have enough. A dozen good searches beats forty shallow ones.

# The labelling discipline

Every factual claim you make about this company falls into exactly one of three
buckets, and you must say which as you write:

- **verified** — you have a source. Name it inline. A URL, or "the job posting".
- **inferred** — you are deriving a pattern from verified facts. Say so, and
  say what it is derived from. Inference is useful; dressing it up as fact is
  not. Treat every inference as a hypothesis the reader tests in the first ten
  minutes of the call, not as a truth.
- **unverified** — plausible, commonly true of companies like this, but you
  could not confirm it. Mark it and move on.

Why this matters, concretely: the reader is going to repeat these facts to a
person who knows whether they are true. A confident wrong number about their
own funding round is worse than saying nothing.

Prefer primary sources — the company's own site, the posting itself, a funding
announcement, a founder's own writing or talk — over aggregators.

If two sources disagree, say so and give both. If a name collides with another
company, say which one you researched and how you can tell them apart.

# Both readings

Where you form a judgement about the company's trajectory, strategy, or
founders, write the charitable read and the uncharitable read, and say plainly
that both can be true at once. Do not resolve it. The reader's job on the call
is to find out which one is closer, and they can only do that if they walk in
holding both.

# What to produce

Research notes. Dense, sourced, organised under headings. This is not the
finished dossier — it is the raw material for one, so favour completeness of
fact over polish of prose. Include:

- Every claim, with its label and its source.
- The requirements the posting states, quoted.
- Whoever the reader will likely meet, and what their background suggests they
  will probe.
- Open questions: the things you could not resolve, phrased as questions to ask
  on the call rather than as gaps in your research.
